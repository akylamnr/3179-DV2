Australian native flora such as Proteaceae, Banksias, Waratahs, and Kangaroo Paws, command strong premiums in overseas hubs like Japan, the United States, and the Netherlands. Their structural durability and long vase life help balance high transit costs.

Distance alone does not determine supplier importance.
Although Asia accounts for the largest combined share of Australia's flower imports, the country-level data tells a different story: Kenya is the largest individual supplier, despite being geographically farther from Australia than its Asian counterparts.

Source: UN Comtrade, Australia imports, HS 060310, calendar years 2017–2024; 2025 from WITS (HS 0603, all cut flowers). Values are reported in US$ million.

The ranking is not fixed. Looking at each year from 2017 to 2025, the order of the leading origins has shifted. Malaysia overtook Kenya in 2021 and led until 2024, but Kenya returned to first in 2025. Vietnam climbed from sixth to second, rising from US$3.2 million to US$20.4 million in import value for fresh flowers.

4. Why does Australia import flowers?

Imports fill the gaps. Flower Industry Australia estimates that around half of the flowers sold in Australia are imported. Three patterns identified in the data can help explain why.

  1. 1Each origin specialises in particular flowers
  2. 2Imports follow calendar events
  3. 3Overseas flowers are cheap to produce

Source: UN Comtrade, Australia imports by partner and product, calendar 2024, HS 060311-060319.

Climate conditions shape regional specialization. Kenya's rose farms include high-altitude sites near Mount Kenya, while Ecuador's rose industry is concentrated in the Andean region around Cayambe, where altitude and intense sunlight favour large blooms and long stems

In Malaysia, Cameron Highlands' temperate climate supports chrysanthemum cultivation, while Vietnam's mild, mountainous Da Lat region is another important flower-growing centre. These growing regions provide context for the specializations visible in Australia's import data, although climate and location alone do not explain trade patterns.


Imports follow the calendar. Across 2021–2025, average monthly import value peaks in February, April, and May (about A$14–15 million, against about A$10 million in most other months). These months include the holidays Valentine's day and Mother's day, which may explain the increase in demand.

Australian cut flowers are grown year-round, with domestic production also peaking around Valentine's Day and Mother's Day. Imports may therefore supplement local supply during these busy periods.


Source: UN Comtrade, HS 0603, 2024. Unit value is import value divided by reported weight.

Labor is the cost behind the price.Australia's imports come mainly from Kenya, Ecuador, China, Malaysia and Colombia, where labor costs are low. Flower Industry Australia says this has contributed to a 40% fall in Australian flower growers over 20 years, and that local growers cannot match what some overseas packers are paid per stem.

5. The Challenges of Domestic Production

Source: ABARES, Labor use in Australian horticulture: Analysis of survey results, 2023–24, Table 2 (CC BY 4.0). Values are survey estimates; RSE is the relative standard error.

Reliance on overseas workers is climbing. Meeting Australia's demand for flowers depends partly on the capacity of domestic growers to maintain production. Labor is one part of this challenge, particularly during peak growing and harvesting periods. ABARES survey estimates show how horticulture farms' use of overseas workers has changed across Australian states.

Nationally, the share of farms using overseas workers in peak months rose from 25.7% to 40.4% between 2021–22 and 2023–24, a rise of 14.7 percentage points. Every state increased. Northern Territory and Tasmania are highest; their estimates are less precise (higher RSE) because they are based on fewer farms.


Source: ABARES, Labor use in Australian horticulture, 2023–24, Table 2.

For overseas workers during peak months, the Northern Territory (62.2%) and Tasmania (57.3%) have the highest reported shares, compared with the national estimate of 40.4%. However, the Northern Territory's estimate is less precise, so it should be interpreted with caution.


Source: ABARES, Labour use in Australian horticulture, 2023–24, Table 3, boundaries: ABS ASGS 2021 SA4. ACT is not surveyed.

Domestic production depends on seasonal labour. Horticulture requires workers throughout the production cycle, but labor needs can rise during peak periods when crops need to be harvested and prepared for market. In 2023–24, Victoria's North West recorded the highest estimated peak-month labor use at 14,100 workers, followed by Melbourne and Mornington Peninsula (11,360) and South Australia's South East (10,220). These regional differences highlight how the workforce supporting Australian horticulture is distributed across the country.

What does this mean for Australia's flower industry? Flowers are part of this wider horticultural system, and their production also depends on having sufficient workers at the right time. Seasonal labor requirements are therefore one consideration when assessing the capacity of domestic growers to supply the market. These workforce estimates cover horticulture broadly, not flower farms alone. They also measure labour use in each region's peak month, rather than total annual employment.

6. Conclusion

Flowers are a small slice of a large industry. Cut flowers make up only a few per cent of horticulture value in every state, and they matter most to Western Australia even though Victoria produces the highest value.

Imports fill the gaps. Roses arrive from Kenya and Ecuador, chrysanthemums from Malaysia and Vietnam, and imports peak around Valentine’s Day and Mother’s Day. Low overseas labour costs help keep them cheap, according to industry sources.

People hold the chain together. On Australian farms labour is a major cost and reliance on overseas workers is rising. Together, the charts show that the flower industry depends on where it grows, what arrives from overseas, and who does the work.
